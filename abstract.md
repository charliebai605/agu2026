MiDAS is a distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) array at Hualien, Taiwan, recording strain-rate along a ~7 km fiber path spanning surface loops and two boreholes (Hole A, ~700 m, crossing the Milun fault; Hole B, ~500 m). Operating since March 2023, it provides dense (4.08 m) spatial sampling and high temporal resolution across depth, offering near-fault deformation views unattainable with conventional networks.

Motivated by continuous seismic-surveillance needs at carbon capture and storage (CCS) and similar sites, we built an automated processing pipeline for MiDAS. It computes STA/LTA RMS energy on borehole channels (1-20 Hz) to detect events, then applies f-k beamforming to classify each by propagation direction (upgoing/downgoing/no moveout signal), isolating true upward arrivals from surface/downhole noise. A complementary long-period (0.1-1 Hz) monitor targets specific depth zones (damage/fault zone) to capture teleseismic surface waves invisible to the 1-20 Hz detector. Daily reports, event catalogs, and depth-resolved RMS profiles are cross-validated against the CWA catalog.

On 8 July 2026, MiDAS recorded a foreshock-mainshock-aftershock sequence: ~12 upgoing foreshocks over 4 hours preceded a local M4.3 mainshock (RMS ~150x background), followed by >50 aftershocks decaying over 7 hours. Separately, Typhoon Bavi's close approach to NE Taiwan (9-11 July 2026) produced a multi-day, ~two-fold rise in background RMS, possibly reflecting storm-generated microseismic noise (not yet verified). The low-frequency zone monitor also detected teleseismic surface waves from regional quakes (e.g., M4.9, Yunnan, China, ~2000 km) absent from the 1-20 Hz detector, whereas comparable, closer events (<500 km) produced energy in both bands -- a preliminary pattern suggesting frequency content may help discriminate source distance.

Together these preliminary observations suggest a single DAS array could serve as a local microseismic network, a regional/teleseismic surface-wave observatory, and an environmental (storm) monitor, pending further verification of the frequency-based distance pattern and the storm attribution. If confirmed, this could offer a low-cost template for separating induced, natural, and environmental signals at future CCS and geohazard-monitoring sites.
