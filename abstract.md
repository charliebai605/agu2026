MiDAS is a distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) array at Hualien, Taiwan, recording strain-rate along a ~7 km fiber path spanning surface loops and two boreholes (Hole A, ~700 m; Hole B, ~500 m) crossing the Milun fault zone. Operating since March 2023, it provides dense (~1 m) spatial sampling and high temporal resolution across depth, offering a view of near-fault deformation unattainable with conventional seismic networks.

Motivated by the need for continuous, automated seismic surveillance at carbon capture and storage (CCS) and similar sites, we built an automated processing pipeline for MiDAS. It computes STA/LTA RMS energy on borehole channels (1-20 Hz) to detect events, then applies f-k beamforming to classify each by propagation direction (upgoing/downgoing/noise), isolating true upward seismic arrivals from surface and downhole noise. A complementary long-period (0.1-1 Hz) monitor targets specific depth zones (damage/fault zone) to capture teleseismic surface waves invisible to the 1-20 Hz detector. Daily reports, event catalogs, and depth-resolved RMS profiles are cross-validated against CWA and USGS catalogs.

The system has captured a range of signals. On 8 July 2026 it recorded a complete foreshock-mainshock-aftershock sequence: ~12 upgoing foreshocks over 4 hours preceded a local M4.3 mainshock (RMS ~150x background), followed by >50 aftershocks decaying over 7 hours. Separately, Typhoon Bavi's close approach to NE Taiwan (9-11 July 2026) produced a multi-day, ~two-fold rise in background RMS consistent with storm-generated ocean-wave microseism. The low-frequency zone monitor also detected teleseismic surface waves from regional quakes (e.g., M4.9, Yunnan, China, ~2000 km) absent from the 1-20 Hz detector, whereas comparable, closer events (<500 km) produced energy in both bands -- showing frequency content alone can discriminate source distance.

Together these observations show that a single DAS array can simultaneously function as a local microseismic network, a regional/teleseismic surface-wave observatory, and an environmental (storm) monitor. This multi-signal discrimination, based only on frequency content and propagation direction, offers a low-cost template for separating induced, natural, and environmental signals at future CCS and geohazard-monitoring sites.
